# Hexa fun principles

Version 1, 2026-11-13. Kept by the market researcher. The creative director reviews designs against this list.
The full study behind it is `2026-11-13-what-makes-strategy-fun.md` (same folder). Source numbers [S1]... refer to its source list.

How to use it: take a design, go down the list, and apply each test. A design does not need to pass every line.
It must not make a line worse without saying why. Where a test needs a number, the number comes from a seeded
full-game run (the method of the economy and trade fun tests).

## The checklist

1. **A real decision every few seasons, all game long.**
   Test: in a fun-test run, a mid-sized country has at least one decision with no obvious answer every 4 seasons, in 1490, 1560 and 1630 alike.
   Sources: Meier's "interesting decisions" [S1]; economy fun test F5 (0 real decisions after 1600) [H1].

2. **Trade-offs, not checklists.** No option is always right, and no option is never right.
   Test: for each option the design adds, name a situation where a good player would pick it, and one where they would not.
   Sources: Meier (decisions have a cost and depend on the situation) [S1]; economy test F3/F4 (Low tax dominant, tile investment dead) [H1]; trade test F7 (tolls dominant) [H2].

3. **Show the price before the choice.**
   Test: every cost and consequence (money, unrest, infamy, losses, payback time) is on the hover card or dialogue before the player commits.
   Sources: Meier ("err on the side of too much information") [S1]; trade test F2 (the order card promised money it could not deliver) [H2]; the stop-steamrolling draft already follows this [H3].

4. **Overlapping goals of different lengths.**
   Test: at any End season, the player has something finishing in 1 to 2 seasons, something in 2 to 5 years, and a long aim.
   Sources: Engelstein's staggered goals and the "one more turn" pull [S2]; research and the build queue already do this early in Hexa.

5. **Every resource always has a next job.**
   Test: the median treasury stays under 15 seasons of income in 1600 and 1649, and no resource sits unused for 10 years.
   Sources: economy test F2 (median hoard 44 seasons of income in 1649) [H1]; forum pattern that grand strategy economies flood by 1600 [S9].

6. **The world pushes back as you grow, for reasons you can name.**
   Test: the leading country (the player included) meets at least one new threat or cost per decade, and the player can say its cause in plain words ("far from the capital", "across the Alps", "a league against us") and see it on the map. A brake that reads as a bare tax fails.
   Sources: designers on snowballing (Civ VI, Humankind, Stellaris, Total War) [S3]; Field of Glory: Empires' decadence, praised for ending the dull late game but called arbitrary by critics [S12]; EU5's complacency [S10]; essay on organic limits [S13]; stop-steamrolling draft [H3].

7. **Each era changes what you do, not only how big the numbers are.**
   Test: each era adds at least one new kind of decision, and keeps the player's identity (their country, their story).
   Sources: Humankind's era change praised for stopping the mid-game slog [S6]; Civ VII's civ switching and era breaks among its top complaints [S5].

8. **Setbacks are stories, not game over.**
   Test: after a lost war, a revolt or a bankruptcy there is a way back within about 10 seasons, and no single event ends a run.
   Sources: Stellaris director on teaching that losing can be fun [S3]; axioms essay (setbacks should hurt but not end the game) [S13].

9. **Remembered moments.** Systems make named stories: battles, generals, places, turning points.
    Test: after a 20-year session, the log or end screen can tell three specific stories with a place and a date.
    Sources: Crusader Kings III's emergent stories are its most praised part [S7]; Old World's family and events [S8].

10. **A goal you can see from the first season.**
    Test: the first screen says how the game ends and how you score; a new aim appears at least once per era.
    Sources: Soren Johnson on victory conditions and Old World's ambitions [S4]; playtest 2026-11-11 ("no stated goal") [H4].

11. **Readable at a glance.**
    Test: a new player can answer "where does my money come from?" and "who is winning this battle?" with one hover.
    Sources: UI is a top complaint for EU5, Civ VII, Old World and Hexarchy [S5, S8, S10, S14].

12. **Less clicking as you grow.**
    Test: clicks per season grow more slowly than decisions per season; the late game has bulk actions or delegation with an override.
    Sources: "Five suggestions for 4X fun" [S15]; CivFanatics essay on complexity outgrowing the interface [S9]; EU5 patch 1.1 bulk army commands [S10].

13. **The AI is a rival, not furniture.**
    Test: the AI uses every system the player uses (trade, peace terms, projects) and still changes the map each decade in a full run.
    Sources: weak AI is a top complaint for Humankind, Millennia and EU5 [S6, S11, S10]; trade test F3 (78 to 88% of AI orders carry nothing) [H2].

14. **Fun in the first 30 minutes.**
    Test: within 5 seasons a new player has made a trade order, a build, a research pick, and seen a threat or a fight.
    Sources: demo and Next Fest advice (players decide in minutes) [S17]; playtest 2026-11-11 (merchants unused for 40 seasons) [H4].

15. **It feels like 1480 to 1650.**
    Test: would this feature look out of place in a generic fantasy 4X? If yes, give it period colour (a real place, date, person type or practice).
    Sources: "Historical" is a top tag on every comparable game's store page [S16]; Hexa's battle cards from real battles [H5].
